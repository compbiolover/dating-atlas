# How the estimates work

The full methods behind the [Dating Atlas](https://datingatlas.dev/) page. The page itself has a short version.

## Population and genders

Population comes from the Census Bureau's 2025 estimates for all 387 metropolitan statistical areas (2023 definitions), taken from [Wikipedia's metro list](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolitan_statistical_area). Counties are mapped to metros with the 2017 delineations ([msacrosswalk](https://github.com/cbgoodman/msacrosswalk)), and a few metros created since then were mapped by hand.

The Census counts only women and men. Among adults of dating age, the page assumes 50% are women, 48.5% are men and 1.5% are nonbinary, close to Pew's 2022 estimate for adults under 50. Nonbinary counts are always modeled.

## Singles and degrees

Singlehood and degree share come from the Census Bureau's American Community Survey for 2019 to 2023. Table B12002 counts never-married, divorced and widowed women and men in 5-year age bands; when a band straddles your age range, the page splits it into single years with a smooth curve that keeps each band's total exactly (a monotone cubic fitted to the running count across band edges), so neighboring years don't jump at band boundaries. The survey can't see unmarried couples who live together. In the 2023 ACS microdata, 26% of single women and 21% of single men aged 25 to 29 live with a partner, against 6% and 12% at 55 to 59, so the page removes that share for each age band and sex. 

Table B15001 gives the share of women and men with a bachelor's degree or higher in five age bands (18 to 24, 25 to 34, 35 to 44, 45 to 64, and 65 and older), and the page uses the band for each year of your age range. Singles hold degrees less often than everyone their age (in the microdata, 0.93 times as often for women aged 25 to 34 and 0.91 times for men, and 0.71 to 0.86 times from 35 up), so each band's share is scaled by that ratio. Nonbinary people get the average of women and men, adjusted by the gaps below. All 387 metros matched an ACS row by name. Small metros have wide margins of error in the ACS, so read their ratios loosely.

If a metro had no ACS row, the page would fall back to 50% single for women (6 points higher for men, 10 for nonbinary people) and to the metro's adult bachelor's rate for degrees, times 1.25 for women, 0.97 for men and 1.15 for nonbinary people.

The adult bachelor's rate in the fallback above is the county share of adults 25 and older with a bachelor's degree or higher, from the 2015 to 2019 ACS ([OpenIntro usdata](https://github.com/OpenIntroStat/usdata)), weighted by population across each metro's counties and scaled up to today's national rate (32.1% to 36.9%). The degree step in the funnel uses Table B15001.

## Who is looking, and for what

Not every single person is available or looking. In Pew's 2022 survey, 20% of people who were neither married nor living with a partner were in a committed relationship ([topline, p. 64](https://www.pewresearch.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/20/2023/01/PI_2023.02.02_Onilne-Dating_FINAL.pdf)), and Pew doesn't break that out by age, so the page removes 20% at every age. Of the rest, Pew's 2019 survey found 67% of men and 61% of women under 40 looking for a relationship or dates, against 55% of men and only 29% of women 40 and older ([report, p. 18](https://www.pewresearch.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/20/2020/08/PSDT_08.20.20.dating-relationships.full_.report.pdf)). By 2022 the share looking had fallen from 61% to 50% for single men and from 38% to 35% for single women ([Pew](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2023/02/08/for-valentines-day-5-facts-about-single-americans/)), so the page scales each age split by that drop: 55% and 45% for men, 56% and 27% for women. Older singles look much less: in the same 2022 survey, 36% of unpartnered adults aged 50 to 64 were looking against 16% of those 65 and older ([Pew](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2023/02/08/for-valentines-day-5-facts-about-single-americans/)), so from 65 the page multiplies the 40-and-older rate by 16/36. Nonbinary people get the average. The same step applies to the competition, since people who aren't looking aren't competing.

"Looking for" removes people who want something different from you. Among singles who were looking in Pew's 2019 survey, 28% wanted only a committed relationship, 20% only casual dates and 53% either: 36%, 16% and 48% of women, 22%, 22% and 56% of men, and 27%, 15% and 58% under 40 against 29%, 24% and 47% from 40 ([Pew](https://www.pewresearch.org/social-trends/2020/08/20/a-profile-of-single-americans/)). Pew doesn't split by sex and age together, so the page scales each sex's share by its age group's share over all daters'. Something serious removes people who want only casual dates, something casual removes those who want only a committed relationship, and either removes no one. Nonbinary people get the average. The competition ratio counts only these people as the ones you're looking for, while competitors still count whatever they want. Pew's split rests on a few hundred daters of each sex, so the range varies the share removed by ±15%.

## Who is open to dating you

How open each group is to dating each gender is a rough national figure from Gallup's 2024 LGBTQ+ identification data for younger adults. About 94% of men and 14% of women are open to dating women, for example. The figures are the same in every metro, so the page won't pick up places with larger queer communities.

## Politics and faith

The values estimate is a model, not survey data. It starts from the 2024 vote, with county results summed for each metro ([tonmcg county results](https://github.com/tonmcg/US_County_Level_Election_Results_08-24)). Connecticut and Alaska don't report by county in that file, so their metros use the statewide Harris share. The politics shares come from how people describe their own views in the [2024 Cooperative Election Study](https://doi.org/10.7910/DVN/X11EP6) (CES): very liberal (shown as progressive), liberal, moderate, conservative, very conservative or not sure (shown as not political). Among single women and men aged 25 to 35 placed in their metros, the page fits how each group's share moves with the metro's Harris share. Single groups are too thin for that, so four broader ones get the fit (left, moderate, right and not political, with one slope for women and men and a shift for women), and left and right split into their two groups by the national ratios. In a metro where Harris got 50%, that gives about 19% progressive, 20% liberal, 29% moderate, 11% conservative, 5% very conservative and 17% not political for women. The left grows and the right shrinks as the Harris share rises. Nonbinary singles (275 in CES, aged 18 to 40) are mostly progressive (59%) or liberal (17%), with too few respondents for a metro fit, so they get those shares everywhere. 

The non-religious share combines two religion sources: `40% + (unaffiliated share − 23.1%) + 0.3 × (48.6% − congregation share)`. The unaffiliated share is the metro's share of adults with no religion, from PRRI's county estimates in its [Census of American Religion](https://www.prri.org/census/), weighted by population across each metro's counties. The congregation share is the share of residents counted as members of a congregation in the [2020 U.S. Religion Census](https://www.usreligioncensus.org/node/1639); metros created since 2020 use the old metro or county they came from. Both figures are metro averages across the atlas (23.1% and 48.6%). The PRRI estimates carry most of the weight because they measure what people say they believe. The congregation share adds how much organized religion is around, but only lightly, since Catholic parishes count everyone they baptized. PRRI's county model already uses education and the vote, so neither enters this formula again. Men are shifted 4 points more non-religious, and nonbinary people 15 points.

Politics and faith can be changed in "Who you're looking for", and you can pick more than one of each. The six politics groups always add to 100%. The faith shares come from the same [PRRI county estimates](https://www.prri.org/census/), weighted by population across each metro's counties: Christian (Catholic, Protestant, Latter-day Saint, Orthodox and Jehovah's Witness), Catholic, Protestant, Latter-day Saint, Jewish, Muslim, Hindu and Buddhist. PRRI has no Pagan or Wiccan estimate, so the page starts from 0.3% of adults ([Pew's 2014 Religious Landscape Study](https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/religious-landscape-study/)) and scales it by each metro's PRRI share of other religions against the atlas average of 1.44%. These are shares of all adults, not of young singles, so they will be off for groups that skew young or old. 

You can pick more than one faith. The groups don't overlap, so the page works out each one with your politics as below and adds them, capped at your politics' share; Any Christian already counts Catholics, Protestants, Latter-day Saints and other Christians (Orthodox, Jehovah's Witness and others, which is what PRRI's Christian share leaves after the three named groups). Christian follows how people describe themselves, not a theological definition. Any non-religious counts atheists, agnostics and people with no religion in particular. PRRI has only the total, so the three parts split it by the shares within each politics group in CES: about 31% atheist, 25% agnostic and 44% nothing in particular among progressives, against 9%, 21% and 70% among conservatives. 

Politics and faith go together more often than chance, so each politics group pairs with no religion and with Christian through its own odds ratio from CES, among single 25 to 35 year olds: for example 2.4 (women) and 2.7 (men) for progressive with no religion, and 4.2 and 2.3 for very conservative with Christian. Worked out this way (a Plackett joint), a pair can never exceed either share on its own. Other faiths pair independently. Degree holders differ: they are more often progressive or liberal (1.4 to 1.5 times), far less often not political (0.3 to 0.4 times), more often Christian (1.2 to 1.3 times odds) and less often unaffiliated, and their politics and faith line up more tightly. So the degree filter uses their shares and odds ratios, with the thinnest of those ratios pulled toward the all-singles ones.

## Diet

The diet step uses national rates. In [Gallup's 2023 poll](https://news.gallup.com/poll/510038/identify-vegetarian-vegan.aspx), 4% of Americans were vegetarian (6% of women, 2% of men) and 1% vegan, and 9% of liberals were vegetarian against 3% of moderates and conservatives, so vegetarian and vegan rates are scaled by 2.25 for progressives and 0.75 for conservatives. Vegan rates split by gender the same way as vegetarian. An [analysis of NHANES](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/nutrition/articles/10.3389/fnut.2023.1113792/full) found 4.7% of adults eat a pescatarian diet, 59.5% of them women; the pescatarian option also counts vegetarians, and only that vegetarian part is scaled by politics. Kosher and halal follow the local faith shares: 17% of Jewish Americans keep kosher at home ([Pew 2020](https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2021/05/11/jewish-practices-and-customs/)), and 83% of Muslim Americans only buy halal or prefer it ([ISPU 2022](https://ispu.org/halal-preferences/)).

## Kids

The kids step removes people whose plans clash with yours. For single adults without kids aged 34 and under, the page uses Pew's 2023 split for 18 to 34 year olds: 45% of women and 57% of men want kids, 30% are unsure, and the rest don't ([Pew](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2024/02/15/among-young-adults-without-children-men-are-more-likely-than-women-to-say-they-want-to-be-parents-someday/)). From 35 up, 47% are counted as not wanting kids, Pew's 2024 figure for adults without kids under 50 who say they're unlikely ever to have them ([Pew](https://www.pewresearch.org/social-trends/2024/07/25/the-experiences-of-u-s-adults-who-dont-have-children/)). The rest are split as at 18 to 34. Pew has no figure for older ages, so 50 and up use the same split. If you want kids, people who don't are removed, and the reverse if you don't; unsure people stay either way. 

If you also say when you want them, the page removes people who want kids on a very different timeline. The [2022–2023 National Survey of Family Growth](https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nsfg/nsfg-2022-2023-puf.htm) asks unmarried people who intend to have a child, and don't live with a partner, when they expect it: within 2 years, in 2 to 5 years, or more than 5 years. Among those without kids, most under 25 expect it more than 5 years out (72% of women, 78% of men), while most from 25 to 34 say 2 to 5 years, and from 35 to 49 35% of women and 19% of men say within 2 years. Wanting kids within 2 years removes the 5-plus group, and wanting them more than 5 years out removes the within-2 group; 2 to 5 years fits either, so it removes no one. NSFG stops at 49, so older ages use the 35 to 49 split, and it has only 37 to 85 effective respondents per band from 35, so those shares are uncertain by about ±5 to 8 points. The range varies the share your timeline removes by ±25%: the relative standard errors of the shares it removes run from 3% to over 50%, mostly 15% to 40%. 

The share who already have kids comes from ACS Table B11012: single mothers and fathers who head a household with their own children under 18, divided by the metro's single adults aged 20 to 64 who don't live with a partner. That misses parents whose kids live mostly with the other parent, so it undercounts fathers.

## Whether they're interested back

"They're interested back" is the rate for someone the same age as them, of average height, with a degree, a steady job and no kids. Your own traits scale it, and each effect is treated as independent:

**Age.** Each person has a largest age gap they'd accept, older and younger. Everyone accepts someone their own age, and the share who'd accept you falls as the gap grows. How the limits change with a person's own age follows [Antfolk (2017)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10367477/), whose sample was 18 to 50; for older people the page extends the same trend, so treat the age effect past 50 as rough. Their size is set so that, on average, the share who wouldn't consider someone 10 years older or younger matches [Pew's 2019 figures](https://www.pewresearch.org/social-trends/2020/08/20/nearly-half-of-u-s-adults-say-dating-has-gotten-harder-for-most-people-in-the-last-10-years/): 27% and 42% of women, 47% and 16% of men.

**Height** applies only between women and men. Women on average want a man about 4 cm taller than them, and men want a woman no taller than them ([Stulp and colleagues, 2013](https://www.gertstulp.com/pdf/Stulp%20et%20al%202013_PAID_Women%20want%20taller%20men%20more%20than%20men%20want%20shorter%20women.pdf)), set against U.S. heights from [NHANES 2015 to 2018](https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/data/series/sr_03/sr03-046-508.pdf). Men's aversion is weaker, so it counts half.

**Education and work.** From the [Survey Center on American Life (2022)](https://www.americansurveycenter.org/research/from-swiping-to-sexting-the-enduring-gender-divide-in-american-dating-and-relationships/), 54% of college-educated women and 44% of college-educated men are less likely to date someone without a degree, and 89% and 66% are less likely to date someone without a job, with lower shares among people without degrees. "Less likely" counts as half a rejection. Pew 2019 found 24% of women and 7% of men wouldn't date someone who makes much less than them.

**Having kids.** Pew 2019 found 42% of singles under 40 and 28% of those 40 and older wouldn't date someone raising children.

These studies are mostly about women and men dating each other, so for nonbinary people the page uses the average of the two.

**Whether it clicks.** The attraction defaults come from speed dating, where people say whether they'd like to see each partner again. Men said yes to 47% and women to 37% of partners in the [Columbia speed-dating data](http://www.stat.columbia.edu/~gelman/arm/examples/speed.dating/) ([Fisman and colleagues, 2006](https://wiwi.hs-duesseldorf.de/personen/christian.schwarz/Documents/genderDifferences.pdf)), and 49% and 34% among 10,526 non-student adults at HurryDate ([Kurzban and Weeden, 2005](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/5252b095e4b06da77026f5e5/t/528d1b22e4b0c1452601cb08/1384979234029/hurrydate-mate-preferences-in-action.pdf)). A yes there only means another conversation, and when people chose after the event instead, in a [UK study](https://www.iser.essex.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/files/working-papers/iser/2007-17.pdf), rates halved to 22% and 12%. So the page uses 35% for men and 25% for women judging someone, and 30% for nonbinary people. Both click sliders depend on who is judging: the first on you, the second on the people you're looking for. In the Columbia data mutual matches came out at about the rate you'd get by multiplying the two, which is how the page combines them. There is no comparable data for same-sex or nonbinary daters.

## On the apps

The app default comes from [Pew's 2022 survey](https://www.pewresearch.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/20/2023/01/PI_2023.02.02_Onilne-Dating_FINAL.pdf): 28% of singles who are looking for dates were using a dating app or site. Pew's past-year figures (50% of looking men, 37% of looking women) give about 31% for men and 23% for women. LGB singles were about 1.5 times as likely to be dating online ([Pew 2019](https://www.pewresearch.org/social-trends/2020/08/20/a-profile-of-single-americans/)), so same-gender and nonbinary matches use 1.5 times those rates. App use has slipped since, with Match Group's paying users down 6% in the year to mid-2026.

These rates are the same in every metro, so the comparison isolates what the metro itself changes. Changing one of these rates scales every gender's rate by the same factor.

## Competition and gender balance

The competition ratio divides the single, looking, degree-holding people of the genders you picked by every single, looking, degree-holding person in the same age range who is open to dating them, whatever their gender. A straight man in a metro with equal numbers of men and women gets about 0.93, because some women also date women. The sex ratio in the list is simpler: single, looking, degree-holding women per man. Because far fewer women than men over 40 are looking, it tilts toward men at older ages. Unchecking the degree filter drops the degree step from your pool and the competition alike.

## The likely range

The range under the main number comes from a Monte Carlo simulation. The page redraws every input 2,000 times from a distribution that reflects how sure we are of it, runs the funnel for each draw, and shows the middle 80% of the results (10th to 90th percentile). 

The spreads come from the sources themselves: the Census Bureau's published margins of error for each metro's single people by age band and degree share in each age band (standard error = margin of error ÷ 1.645, summed across bands as root sum of squares); survey sample sizes for Gallup's openness rates and PRRI's faith shares; ±8% for Pew's share of singles who are looking, ±15% on the share your relationship goal removes and ±25% on the share your kids timeline removes, ±3.5% for the cohabiting cut; ±15% on the share of your politics groups (CES puts each group's sampling error at 6% to 15%, and the metro fit adds more) and ±5 points on the non-religious base, a modeling choice; ±24% on the odds ratios between politics and faith (±38% among degree holders) and ±10% on how a degree shifts faith, from bootstrap resamples of the survey; ±25% each on the two attraction rates, which spans the published yes-rates from 22% to 49%; ±6% on the kids step; and ±5% to ±30% on the diet rates, depending on the survey. The page also reports which group of inputs drives most of the range, from how much each varies the result on its own. The range only covers uncertainty in the inputs. It does not cover errors in how the funnel is built, like assuming the steps are independent.

## Over a year

The main number is one moment. Over a year, new people join the pool, so the page also shows how many would be possibilities at some point in the next 12 months: today's pool plus everyone who joins it. For each year of age in your range, weighted by how many single people are looking at that age, it adds per single person not living with a partner:

- **Divorced or widowed in the past year.** From the 2023 ACS 1-year microdata: the share of single people not living with a partner who divorced or were widowed in the past 12 months (`MARHD`, `MARHW`), by sex and age band (`PUMS.div`). About 2% a year for women 30 to 34 and 4 to 5% for women in their 40s.
- **Moved here in the past year.** Each metro's share of residents who lived in another metro, a micro area, outside both or abroad a year earlier (ACS 2019 to 2023, Table B07201, `MOVE`): 2% in New York, 7% in Austin, 18% in Ithaca. Singles in their twenties move far more often than everyone else, so that share is scaled by how often single people of your sex and age band moved from another state or abroad, against everyone (`PUMS.mig`, about 2 times as often at 25 to 29). That assumes moves between metros follow the same age pattern as moves between states.
- **Breakups.** Pew counts 20% of singles as already in a committed relationship, which makes 0.25 of them per unattached single; those relationships end at 30% a year. [Rhoades et al. (2011)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3115386/) found that 36.5% of unmarried 18-to-35-year-olds in a relationship broke up at least once in 20 months, about 24% a year, including couples who lived together; couples who only date break up more often, hence 30%. People living with a partner (from `PUMS.free`) split at 15% a year: in the [2006 to 2010 NSFG](https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/data/nhsr/nhsr064.pdf), within 3 years of starting, 27% of first premarital cohabitations had dissolved, 40% had turned into marriages and 32% were intact, and constant yearly rates of breaking up and marrying give that split at 15% and 23%.

People who join are taken to look, fit and click at the same rates as everyone else in their gender and age. The count leaves out single people who weren't looking and start, people aging into your range (it moves with your age) and anyone who joins and leaves again within the year, so it's a floor. Breakup rates for people over 35 are carried over from younger ones, since neither source covers them. In the range, the two breakup rates vary together by ±35%; the ACS shares are taken as known.

For most searches, a year adds 20% to 30% to today's count in 8 of 10 metros, from about 17% at the low end to nearly half in college towns: about 20% in New York and Memphis, 26% in Austin and 43% in Ithaca for the default search.

## If you're still looking later

This chart reruns the whole funnel with your age and the ages you're looking for one year older each time, for up to 10 years, with everything else as you set it. It mostly moves because fewer people are single in their 30s than their 20s, more are divorced or widowed later on, fewer are looking after 40 and again after 65, and age gaps change how open people are to you. It compares today's older and younger people (a cross-section), not one group of people followed over time, so it misses how today's 30-year-olds will differ from today's 40-year-olds when they get there, and how the metro itself will change. It's a rough guide to what changes with age, not a forecast.

## Map, rent and climate

The map puts each metro at its internal point from the Census Bureau's [2023 Gazetteer](https://www.census.gov/geographies/reference-files/time-series/geo/gazetteer-files.html), with state outlines from [us-atlas](https://github.com/topojson/us-atlas), in the Albers USA projection that moves Alaska and Hawaii into insets. Circle area is proportional to the number of possibilities on the apps right now.

Housing cost is a recent mover's rent for a 1-bedroom as a share of median yearly pay, from the 2019 to 2023 ACS. Table B25031 gives each metro's median gross rent, including utilities, for a 1-bedroom. That median includes people on long leases and in rent-stabilized units, so it is scaled by the metro's ratio of rent for people who moved in from 2021 on to rent for all renters (Table B25113). Pay is the median earnings of people 25 and older with a bachelor's degree, or with some college if you set no bachelor's (Table B20004), and it covers part-time workers too. The 30% line is HUD's definition of a cost-burdened renter. Rents have risen since 2023, so treat the dollar figures as a floor. The ranking between metros holds up better. Housing cost doesn't change the pool. It's a separate measure of what living there costs.

Climate comes from NOAA's county temperatures ([nClimDiv](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/pub/data/cirs/climdiv/)): each county's average January and July temperature over 1991 to 2020, NOAA's current normals period, weighted by population across each metro's counties. Warm winters means a January average of 50°F or warmer (Miami, Los Angeles, Houston, Phoenix), cold, snowy winters means 30°F or colder (Minneapolis, Chicago, Boston, Salt Lake City), and four seasons is everything between. These are averages over whole counties, so metros whose counties reach into mountains read colder than the city itself: Seattle's January is 36°F here, because King and Pierce counties take in part of the Cascades. NOAA has no separate figure for the District of Columbia, so the Washington metro uses its other counties. Like housing cost, climate doesn't change the pool. It only picks which metros the map, the ranked list and the comparisons show.
