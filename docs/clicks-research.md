# Split the click sliders by rater gender

The two attraction sliders should follow the gender of the person judging, and the app slider is set against the wrong base. In real-world first-impression data, **men say yes to about 47–49% of women and women to about 34–37% of men**. Once you discount from "willing to talk again" to "want to date", the defaults come out at **about 35% for men rating women and about 25% for women rating men**. Under that rule, "They're interested back" defaults to **about 25% for a male user and about 35% for a female user**. The product of the two sliders is **about 8.75% for either gender**, close to the current pooled 30% × 30% = 9%. The split therefore changes what users see on each slider more than it changes the mutual-interest total for opposite-sex pairs. In the Columbia speed-dating data, the mutual-match rate is 95% of what independence predicts, so multiplying the two sliders is sound. The app slider has the larger problem. Pew's 28% counts only singles who are looking, and the atlas pool includes every single person. Against all singles, the evidence supports **about 14–15% (men about 20%, women about 11–15%)**. The current 35% default overstates app reach about 2.3 times. Every dataset behind the attraction numbers is heterosexual. Same-sex values below are extrapolations, and there is no data at all for nonbinary daters.

## Recommended settings at a glance

| Slider | Current | Recommended default | Recommended range | Main anchors |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. You're attracted to them, rater is a man | 30%, 5–60% | **35%** | 10–65% | Speed-dating yes 47–49%; share of partners he rates 8 or more out of 10 for looks: 29%; UK post-event proposals 22% |
| 1. You're attracted to them, rater is a woman | 30%, 5–60% | **25%** | 5–55% | Speed-dating yes 34–37%; share rated 8 or more: 22%; UK post-event proposals 12% |
| 2. They're interested back, male user (women judging) | 30%, 5–60% | **25%** | 5–55% | Same evidence as women raters above; women's first-message reply rates 16–20% |
| 2. They're interested back, female user (men judging) | 30%, 5–60% | **35%** | 10–65% | Same evidence as men raters above; men's reply rates of about 26–32% |
| 3. On the apps, base = all singles (as the atlas is built) | 35%, 10–60% | **15%** (men about 20%, women about 13%) | 5–35% | Pew 2022: 28% of looking singles and 3% of not-looking singles are current users |
| 3. On the apps, base = singles who are looking (only if a "looking" step is added) | — | **28%** (men about 31%, women about 23%) | 15–45% | Pew 2022, with trend and gender adjustments |

The gender splits for slider 3 are my own derivations from published Pew totals, explained below. Pew does not publish them directly.

## Speed dating puts men's yes rate near 48% and women's near 36%

The closest real-world measure of slider 1 is the speed-dating scorecard. After a few minutes in person, each person ticks whether they would like to see that partner again. The pools are age-matched and mostly educated, which suits the atlas. In the public Columbia dataset (21 waves, 2002–2004, 551 graduate students, 8,378 ratings), **women said yes to 36.5% of men and men to 47.4% of women**, or 42.0% pooled ([Columbia stat dataset](http://www.stat.columbia.edu/~gelman/arm/examples/speed.dating/)). The published Columbia paper reports a pooled decision mean of 0.43 ([Fisman et al. 2006](https://wiwi.hs-duesseldorf.de/personen/christian.schwarz/Documents/genderDifferences.pdf)). The largest non-student sample is US HurryDate (2003): 10,526 participants, mean age 31–34, about 17 years of schooling. It reports nearly the same split: **men were chosen by 34% of women, and women by 49% of men** ([Kurzban & Weeden 2005](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/5252b095e4b06da77026f5e5/t/528d1b22e4b0c1452601cb08/1384979234029/hurrydate-mate-preferences-in-action.pdf)). A UK agency with 84 events and mean age around 35 is the low outlier. There, people chose after the event with partners' profiles in hand. Women chose 2.6 of about 22 men and men chose 5, which works out to **about 12% for women and 22% for men** ([Belot & Francesconi](https://www.iser.essex.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/files/working-papers/iser/2007-17.pdf)).

Part of the gender gap comes from how events are set up, but not all of it. In Columbia sessions with fewer than 15 partners, both sexes said yes to about half. In larger sessions women became more selective, by 1.3 points per extra partner, while men did not ([Fisman et al. 2006](https://wiwi.hs-duesseldorf.de/personen/christian.schwarz/Documents/genderDifferences.pdf)). Whoever rotates between tables is less picky: when women rotated, the usual pattern of men being less selective disappeared ([Finkel & Eastwick 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19754525/)). Even so, the two large samples that use standard formats agree on a men-to-women ratio of about 1.3–1.4. The rater's own age and education each explained under 1% of selectivity in HurryDate ([Kurzban & Weeden 2005](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/5252b095e4b06da77026f5e5/t/528d1b22e4b0c1452601cb08/1384979234029/hurrydate-mate-preferences-in-action.pdf)). In the Columbia data, yes rates did not fall with age up to 55 (women 45% and men 49% in the 31–55 band). So one gender-specific default across 21–55 is defensible, although there are few raters over 45 in any of these samples.

A speed-dating yes overstates the slider, because a yes means "willing to talk again", not "want to date". Only **about 36% of Columbia respondents had been on a date with any of their matches within a month** ([Columbia stat dataset](http://www.stat.columbia.edu/~gelman/arm/examples/speed.dating/)). A cross-check using the Columbia attractiveness ratings sets a floor: raters scored **29% (men) and 22% (women)** of partners at 8 or more out of 10, and 49% and 41% at 7 or more. Among partners rated 7, 51–60% got a yes. The defaults of 35% for men and 25% for women sit between the raw yes rate (the ceiling) and the 8-or-more share and UK deliberated rate (the floor). These are my judgements; no source measures "share of otherwise-eligible partners one would want to date" directly.

## App swipe data overstate the gender gap

Dating-app like rates show a much wider gap: **men about 33–53%, women about 5–14%**. This comes from a 2014 unnamed-source report of 46% and 14% on Tinder ([TIME](http://time.com/3547890/tinder-men-women-swipe-right/)), self-uploaded Tinder exports (men about 53%, women about 5%) ([SwipeStats](https://swipestats.io/blog/dating-app-statistics-gender)), and Hinge exports (men 33%, women 6%) ([SwipeStats Hinge](https://www.swipestats.io/blog/hinge-statistics)). Neither number fits the slider. Men's rate is inflated by strategy: in one survey, **33% of men "casually like most profiles", against 0% of women** ([Tyson et al. 2016](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1607.01952)). Women's rate is deflated by the pool. Raw app feeds include people outside a user's age, height and other deal-breakers, which users screen out before engaging ([Bruch et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5035909)). The atlas has already removed those people. App data still serve two purposes. They confirm the direction of the gap. And because 93% of women say they like only profiles they find attractive, they set a floor for women at about 5% ([Tyson et al. 2016](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1607.01952)), which supports keeping the 5% bottom of the range for women raters.

For slider 2, message reply rates are the app-side check. On a large US site in 2014, **women replied to under 20% of first messages**, and men writing to women more desirable than themselves never got above 21% ([Bruch & Newman 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6082652/)). Older site data put first-contact replies at about 16% from women and 26% from men, but that figure comes from a secondary summary I could not check against the paper ([Psychology Today](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/love-digitally/201609/what-to-say-and-not-to-in-a-first-online-dating-message)). OkCupid's 2009 straight reply rate was 32% ([OkTrends mirror](https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/okcupid/onlinedatingadvicesamesexmessaging.html)). These rates understate interest from peers, because people aim their messages at partners about 25% more desirable than themselves ([Bruch & Newman 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6082652/)). The atlas user is average, so these rates are a floor under the speed-dating values, not a replacement for them.

## "Interested back" mirrors the other gender's attraction rate

For an average user, slider 2 is the other gender's slider 1 measured on the user. The defaults should therefore be **25% for a male user (women judging) and 35% for a female user (men judging)**. The user profile in slider 2 (same age, average height, degree, job, no children) is roughly average within a college-educated pool, so no premium is warranted. The UK data put a number on the traits that move this slider. Five extra centimetres of height raised a man's proposals from women by about 9%. More educated women were 10% more likely to receive proposals. Being overweight cut a woman's proposals by about 60% ([Belot & Francesconi](https://www.iser.essex.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/files/working-papers/iser/2007-17.pdf)). The range has to cover how unevenly yeses are spread. In HurryDate, the standard deviation of the share of yeses a person received was **21–22 points** ([Kurzban & Weeden 2005](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/5252b095e4b06da77026f5e5/t/528d1b22e4b0c1452601cb08/1384979234029/hurrydate-mate-preferences-in-action.pdf)). On Hinge, half of all likes to men went to the top 15% of men ([Global Dating Insights](https://www.globaldatinginsights.com/from-the-web/hinge-growth-engineer-explains-how-likes-are-distributed-on-the-dating-app/)). One standard deviation either side of each default gives about 5–50% for men being judged and 15–60% for women being judged. Hence the recommended ranges of 5–55% (male user) and 10–65% (female user).

## Multiply the two sliders; the Columbia match rate sits at independence

In the Columbia data, **16.5% of pairs matched, against 17.3% if the two decisions were independent** (0.365 × 0.474). That is a ratio of 0.95 ([Columbia stat dataset](http://www.stat.columbia.edu/~gelman/arm/examples/speed.dating/)). Two forces push the product slightly too high. People who received more yeses gave fewer yeses (correlations of .18–.21) ([Kurzban & Weeden 2005](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/5252b095e4b06da77026f5e5/t/528d1b22e4b0c1452601cb08/1384979234029/hurrydate-mate-preferences-in-action.pdf)). And people tend to want partners above their own desirability, who reply less often ([Bruch & Newman 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6082652/)). The net effect is about 5%, smaller than the wave-to-wave spread in match rates (8–31%). Plain multiplication is fine. If the atlas wants to absorb that effect, it can apply an optional 0.95 factor. Splitting by gender leaves opposite-sex mutual interest unchanged: 35% × 25% = **8.75%** whichever partner is the user, against 9% under the pooled 30/30. The split is about showing honest per-slider numbers. It barely moves the headline count for opposite-sex pairs.

## The app slider is set against the wrong base

Pew's July 2022 survey (N=6,034) found that **28% of single adults who are looking were currently using a dating site or app**, and 45% had used one in the past year. Among singles who are not looking, **only 3% were current users** ([Pew 2023 PDF, p. 20](https://www.pewresearch.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/20/2023/01/PI_2023.02.02_Onilne-Dating_FINAL.pdf)). Only **43% of unpartnered adults were looking** in 2022 (50% in 2019). Looking singles were 13% of all adults and singles 30% ([Pew topline, pp. 65–66](https://www.pewresearch.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/20/2023/01/PI_2023.02.02_Onilne-Dating_FINAL.pdf)). The atlas filters to singles but never to "looking", so slider 3 is applied to all singles. Weighting the two groups gives (0.13 × 28% + 0.17 × 3%) / 0.30 = **about 14% of all singles on apps right now**. The current 35% default is about 2.5 times that, and the 10% floor sits near the true central value. The atlas has two coherent fixes. It can rebase slider 3 to all singles, with a default of about 15% and a range of 5–35%. Or it can add an explicit "looking to date" step and keep slider 3 at about 28%. The second fix applies the "looking" filter once, through its own step, rather than hiding it inside the app slider. The first fix is simpler and matches the page as it stands.

Gender and age matter for this slider. In 2019, **61% of single men and 38% of single women were looking**. Under 40 the gap is small (67% versus 61%), but over 40 it is wide (58% versus 29%) ([Pew 2020](https://www.pewresearch.org/social-trends/2020/08/20/a-profile-of-single-americans/)). Pew publishes past-year use among looking singles by gender (men 50%, women 37%) but not current use. Applying the overall current-to-past-year ratio (28/45 ≈ 0.62) gives estimated current use of about 31% for looking men and 23% for looking women. Combining that with the looking shares gives **about 20% of all single men and 11–15% of all single women** on apps. The 11% comes from all-age looking shares and the 15% from under-40 shares. These are my estimates from Pew totals, not published figures. Two smaller adjustments follow. A college degree barely matters: 9% of graduates were current or past-year users, against 13% of people with some college ([Pew 2023 PDF](https://www.pewresearch.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/20/2023/01/PI_2023.02.02_Onilne-Dating_FINAL.pdf)). The trend points slightly down. Match Group payers fell to 13.3 million by Q2 2026, Tinder payers fell 7–8% a year, while Hinge active users grew 13% ([Match 8-K, Aug 2026](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0000891103/000089110326000128/mtch8-k20260804ex991.htm)). That argues for keeping defaults at or below the 2022 level. As a cross-check, SSRS found **7% of all US adults currently use online dating** in February 2025 ([SSRS 2025](https://ssrs.com/insights/the-public-and-online-dating-in-2025/)). That caps app use among singles at about 23% even if every user were single, which brackets the 15% default from above.

## Same-sex and nonbinary daters rest on extrapolation

None of the speed-dating datasets includes same-sex daters. HurryDate dropped same-sex matches as data errors ([Kurzban & Weeden 2005](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/5252b095e4b06da77026f5e5/t/528d1b22e4b0c1452601cb08/1384979234029/hurrydate-mate-preferences-in-action.pdf)), and a PubMed search found no same-sex yes rates. The app evidence suggests that selectivity follows the rater's gender more than orientation. In one Tinder study, **86% of the matches a fake male profile received came from men**, and the authors concluded gay men "are far more active in liking than heterosexual women" ([Tyson et al. 2016](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1607.01952)). OkCupid's same-sex reply rate was **41%, against 32% for straight messages** ([OkTrends mirror](https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/okcupid/onlinedatingadvicesamesexmessaging.html)). The practical rule is to set each slider by the gender of whoever is judging. For gay men, use 35% on both sliders, giving a 12.25% mutual rate. For lesbian women, use 25% on both, giving 6.25%. The lesbian figure has no direct support and could be too low, since the women's numbers come entirely from women judging men. There is no data for nonbinary daters, so use the pooled 30% on both sliders and keep the full 5–60% range. App use is clearly higher for LGB singles. In 2022, 13% of all LGB adults were current users, against 4% of straight adults. Among single adults in 2019, 37% of LGB singles were currently online dating, against 24% of straight singles ([Pew 2023 PDF](https://www.pewresearch.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/20/2023/01/PI_2023.02.02_Onilne-Dating_FINAL.pdf); [Pew 2020](https://www.pewresearch.org/social-trends/2020/08/20/a-profile-of-single-americans/)). Pew's 2019 wording does not say whether that base is all singles or looking singles. Either way, a multiplier of **about 1.5 on slider 3 for LGB users** (about 22% against an all-singles base) is supported. About 6 in 10 gay and bisexual men who date online have used Grindr ([Pew 2023](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2023/06/26/about-half-of-lesbian-gay-and-bisexual-adults-have-used-online-dating/markdown)), so their "on the apps" figure covers a different set of apps.

## Conclusion

The biggest correction is to slider 3's base, not to the attraction sliders. Moving from 35% to about 15% halves the estimated app-reachable pool. By comparison, splitting sliders 1 and 2 by gender leaves the opposite-sex mutual rate almost unchanged. The atlas's opposite-sex headline numbers were therefore mainly overstated by the app slider. Splitting by gender makes each slider honest about who is judging, and it gives a principled way to set same-sex defaults. It also makes clear that slider 2 is not a separate quantity: it is the other gender's slider 1 applied to the user.

The evidence has three weak points, and these are where better data would most change the defaults. The attraction anchors date from 2002–2005, when people met through in-person scorecards rather than apps. There is almost no data on raters aged 45–55 or on lesbian and nonbinary daters. And no survey since July 2022 measures current app use among singles who are looking. If Pew repeats its online-dating survey, the first figure to update is the 28%, along with current-use figures by gender and age, which Pew has not yet published.
